Human aging is an unsolved evolutionary puzzle

Humans are extraordinarily long-lived compared to other mammals. To determine whether our extreme lifespan is a general primate adaptation or a unique anomaly, we must establish the baseline aging rules of our closest living relatives: the great apes and lesser apes.

Great apes outlive lesser apes by 14.6 years

The evolutionary split between lesser apes (gibbons) and great apes (hominids) brought a major shift in lifespan. There is virtually no overlap between the two families; the shortest-lived great ape outlives nearly all gibbon species.

But body mass explains this entire longevity gap

Great apes did not evolve a novel biological mechanism for slower aging. They simply grew ten times larger. In evolutionary biology, larger animals scale to live longer. Body mass alone explains nearly half the lifespan variance across these hominoid species.

Human lifespan is decoupled from this scaling rule

Humans weigh 62 kg, placing us squarely within the normal great ape mass distribution. Yet, even using a highly conservative historical maximum lifespan of 90 years, humans outlive our biological prediction by over three decades. We age entirely outside the established hominoid boundaries.

Human aging requires a unique evolutionary explanation

  • The human longevity anomaly cannot be explained by our physical size or our membership in the great ape clade.
  • Our extended lifespan represents a distinct, species-specific evolutionary trajectory.
  • This decoupling from baseline hominoid allometry suggests alternative evolutionary drivers must be responsible for our slowed rate of aging.

Three specific next experiments

  • Regress longevity against encephalization (brain/body mass) across primates to test neurological drivers.
  • Investigate baseline metabolic rates (W) to test the "rate of living" theory in long-lived outliers.
  • Apply Phylogenetic Generalized Least Squares to partition allometry from shared ancestry.
