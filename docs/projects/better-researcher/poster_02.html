The "fast-slow" continuum is constrained by physical anatomy

How do divergent biomechanical realities—like aerial oviparity versus terrestrial viviparity—alter life-history trade-offs in apex predators?

All organisms trade somatic maintenance against reproduction. We compared 20 avian (Accipitriformes) and 16 mammalian (Canidae) predators to quantify lineage-specific life-history scaling.

Avian predators gain lifespan rapidly as body mass increases

Does a predominantly aerial lifestyle allow avian lineages to delay senescence?

Yes. Body mass strictly predicts maximum longevity in birds, but is a weak predictor in mammals. The avian lineage exhibits significantly enhanced somatic maintenance at macroscopic sizes.

Longevity scales 3x faster in birds

Above 46g, Accipitriformes gain lifespan significantly faster than Canidae (interaction p=0.002). Avian R²=0.68; Mammalian R²=0.14.

Avian longevity is financed by severely restricted reproduction

What is the reproductive penalty for this extended survival, and how does mass affect it?

Extended survival requires diverted energy. Unlike terrestrial viviparity, the aerodynamic burden of carrying eggs imposes strict limits on the reproductive capacity of heavy birds.

Birds produce half as many offspring

Absolute annual fecundity is significantly lower in Accipitriformes (mean 2.8) than Canidae (mean 5.4) (p<0.001).

Mass strictly limits avian reproduction

Avian fecundity scales negatively with mass (slope -0.15, p=0.02), while mammalian fecundity is unconstrained (slope 0.04, p=0.61).

Heavy birds face severe developmental delays

How do these competing constraints alter developmental timelines?

Adding mass delays reproduction in birds far more severely than in mammals. The prolonged development necessary for aerial hunting likely requires near-adult skeletal ossification before independent survival.

Avian maturation delays rapidly with mass

Female maturity scales significantly more steeply with mass in birds (slope 0.38) than in mammals (slope 0.15) (interaction p=0.021).

Avian and mammalian lineages follow strictly divergent trajectories

  • Above 46g, avian predators gain longevity significantly faster per unit mass than mammals.
  • Avian fecundity scales negatively with mass, whereas mammalian fecundity remains independent.
  • Avian maturation is strictly delayed and scales steeply with mass.
  • Accipitriformes operate on a highly mass-constrained "slow" trajectory compared to the decoupled "fast" trajectory of Canidae.

Next steps to isolate causal biomechanical drivers

  • Incorporate flightless birds and flying mammals to separate the effects of flight from broader phylogenetic constraints.
  • Evaluate weaning and fledging times to partition dependent sub-adult phases.
  • Calculate total lifetime energetic investment to accurately compare oviparity and gestation costs.

Data & Methods

AnAge database (N=36 strictly filtered species). Continuous variables log10-transformed. Tested via OLS regression and ANCOVA.

Limitations

Two-lineage comparison introduces phylogenetic confounds. Raw offspring count is an imperfect proxy for total energetic investment.
